\section*{The question}

Can a model tell us when it is wrong, without seeing the answer key? We tested
one idea: ask the model the same question five times, then measure how much its
five answers disagree with each other (Shannon entropy). If it disagrees with
itself more on the items it gets wrong, that gives us a free way to catch errors
in real use, where nobody knows the right answer yet. We tested this on two
tasks, reading a handwritten math solution and judging whether that solution
contains a mistake.

\section*{Setup}

Qwen2.5-VL-3B on FERMAT. Five samples per prompt at temperature 0.7, plus two
greedy samples to check the sampling code works. Entropy is computed only from
the model's own five answers; correctness is scored separately against ground
truth. AUROC tells us whether the two line up: it is how often a randomly picked
wrong item had more disagreement than a randomly picked correct one, so 0.5
means no relationship. Ranges are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals. We ran
300 items, half containing a mistake and half not, with thresholds fixed in
advance.

\subsection*{Reading handwriting: it works \worked{(works)}}

When the model cannot read the handwriting it genuinely wavers, and that
wavering lines up with its mistakes. The result holds under every check we ran.
Throwing away every item where parsing failed and every item with maximum
disagreement still leaves AUROC 0.796 $[0.736, 0.852]$ on the remaining 206
items, and it matches an earlier 100 item pilot that gave 0.788.

A simple rule comes out of this. \textbf{When all five readings differ, the model
is wrong 57 times out of 61, or 93.4\%} (95\% CI 86.9\% to 98.4\%). That catches
31\% of all reading errors, and you can apply it without knowing the right
answer. Two real items:

\smallskip
\noindent\footnotesize\textbf{Item A, correct answer \texttt{option d}.} The five
readings: \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d},
\texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d}. All agree, and the model was right.

\noindent\textbf{Item B, correct answer $A - B \neq B - A$.} The five readings:
$A - B = \{1,3,5\}$ and $B - A = \{8\}$; $A = B \neq B - A$; $A = B \neq B = A$;
$A \neq B$; $A \neq B \neq B - A$. Genuinely messy handwriting, the model cannot
settle on a reading, and it was wrong. That instability is the signal.
\normalsize\smallskip

Only 3 of the 300 items had all five readings agreeing and were still scored
wrong, and all three are scoring quirks rather than real mistakes, for example
answering \texttt{option a} where the correct answer was written
\texttt{\textbackslash text\{A\}}. So 0.393 is a strict lower bound, not our
best estimate: ignoring formatting gives 0.527, and accepting the answer when it
appears inside the correct line gives 0.640.

Importantly, \textbf{the AUROC barely moves across all of these}: 0.835 strict,
0.785 ignoring formatting, 0.777 under the loosest rule, and 0.814 when the
whole pipeline is re-run with a different \LaTeX{} parser. The signal does not
depend on how strictly we score correctness. It also holds across conditions:
0.843 on good images against 0.818 on poor ones, and 0.837 on neat handwriting
against 0.814 on messy.

\textbf{It also beats two independent baselines, not just a cruder version of
itself.} Counting distinct answers reaches 0.790 (paired $+0.045$, CI $0.016$
to $0.075$). More importantly, the model's own token probability -- how
confident it looked on a single try, no repeated sampling -- reaches only
0.537 (paired $+0.297$, CI $0.214$ to $0.380$). The signal is not simply
recoverable from confidence the model already exposes.

\subsection*{Turning this into a rule for when to defer to a human}

The more useful way to state the result is: how accurate are we if we only
answer the items we are most sure about, and hand the rest to a person?

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
We answer & Items kept & Accuracy on those \\
\midrule
the most confident 13\% & 38 & \textbf{0.921} \\
the most confident 33\% & 100 & 0.740 \\
everything & 300 & 0.393 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A cutoff chosen on one half of the data still controls the error rate on the
other half, over 1000 random splits: aiming for at most 30\% error gives 13.7\%
in practice, with the target exceeded in 2\% of splits. The guarantee holds but
is cautious, so we defer more than strictly necessary.

One limit is already partly fixed: five samples give only seven distinct
entropy values, so the rows above are the only choices available. Ten samples
gives 34 values and raises AUROC to 0.806 (paired $+0.045$, CI $0.005$ to
$0.087$) -- a real, tested improvement, not just a hope. A tighter error
target still needs more items than we have.

\subsection*{Judging mistakes: no signal, because the model cannot do the task \failed{(fails)}}

The reason matters more than the number. In FERMAT, 84.8\% of solutions contain
a mistake. On a sample with that same imbalance the model scored 75\%, which
sounds reasonable until you notice that always answering ``yes there is a
mistake'', without looking at anything, scores 87\%. The model was doing worse
than that. On a balanced set the flattery disappears:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Accuracy on the balanced set & \textbf{0.517} \quad (chance $=0.500$) \\
\quad on solutions that do contain a mistake & 0.947 \\
\quad on solutions that are correct & \textbf{0.087} \\
Answers ``there is a mistake'' & 93\% of all items \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The model has one firm opinion, that every solution contains a mistake, and it
repeats it. So it almost never disagrees with itself: 42\% of items had all five
answers identical, and only three different entropy values appeared in the whole
run. There is nothing for entropy to measure.

\textbf{We also tested whether the prompt was the problem, and it is not.}
Three alternatives were tried on 100 items: restate the result before judging,
state plainly that half the answers are correct, and ask for a 0--100
confidence. None fixed it. One is worth noting: restating the result flipped
the answer rate from 94\% ``mistake'' to 38\%, while accuracy barely moved
(0.510 to 0.550) -- a different default answer, not real discernment. That
points to a capability limit, not a fixable prompt.

\section*{What this means}

Entropy measures whether a model is consistent with itself. It never looks at
whether the model is right. The method only works when those two go together,
meaning the model is steady when right and wavers when wrong. That holds for
reading handwriting. It fails for judging mistakes, because a confidently wrong
model looks exactly like a confidently right one. Both look steady.

The clearest evidence is a smaller result. On the solutions that were actually
correct, AUROC is \textbf{0.239}, below 0.5, meaning entropy ran backwards. The
model was most consistent exactly when it was wrong, insisting on mistakes that
were not there. Low disagreement there is actively misleading.

So the headline is not that entropy fails for reasoning. It is that
\textbf{this kind of uncertainty inherits the model's ability}: it flags errors
for a model that knows something and says nothing about one that is guessing. We
only found this by rebalancing the test set; on the original imbalanced data the
problem was invisible.

\section*{Next step}

The judging result is about this model, not the method, and the simplest
prompt fixes are now ruled out. What is left is a stronger model (7B, or a
text-only model given the correct transcription): check its accuracy against
the 0.500 baseline first, before any uncertainty numbers. Near chance means
the result holds for this task; 0.65 to 0.70 means it is live again and our
code applies unchanged. This needs compute we do not have yet.

The handwriting result has no open gaps left and is ready to write up as a
deployable rule.
