\section*{The question}

Can a model tell us when it is wrong, without seeing the answer key? We tested
one idea. Ask the model the same question five times, then measure how much its
five answers disagree with each other (Shannon entropy). If the model disagrees
with itself more on the items it gets wrong, that gives us a free way to catch
errors in real use, where nobody knows the right answer yet.

We tested this on two tasks: reading a handwritten math solution, and judging
whether that solution contains a mistake.

\section*{Setup}

Qwen2.5-VL-3B on FERMAT. Five samples per prompt at temperature 0.7, plus two
greedy samples to check the sampling code works. Entropy is computed only from
the model's own five answers; correctness is scored separately against ground
truth. AUROC tells us whether the two line up: it is how often a randomly picked
wrong item had more disagreement than a randomly picked correct one, so 0.5
means no relationship. Ranges are 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals. We ran
300 items, half containing a mistake and half not, with thresholds fixed in
advance.

\subsection*{Reading handwriting: it works \worked{(works)}}

When the model cannot read the handwriting it genuinely wavers, and that
wavering lines up with its mistakes. The result holds under every check we ran.
Throwing away every item where parsing failed and every item with maximum
disagreement still leaves AUROC 0.796 $[0.736, 0.852]$ on the remaining 206
items, and it matches an earlier 100 item pilot that gave 0.788.

A simple rule comes out of this. \textbf{When all five readings differ, the model
is wrong 57 times out of 61, or 93.4\%.} That catches 31\% of all reading
errors, and you can apply it without knowing the right answer. Two real items:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.15\linewidth}p{0.80\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Five identical readings, and correct}} \\
\midrule
correct answer & \texttt{option d} \\
5 readings & \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d}, \texttt{option d} \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Five different readings, and wrong}} \\
\midrule
correct answer & $A - B \neq B - A$ \\
5 readings & $A - B = \{1,3,5\}$ and $B - A = \{8\}$ \quad|\quad $A = B \neq B - A$ \quad|\quad
             $A = B \neq B = A$ \quad|\quad $A \neq B$ \quad|\quad $A \neq B \neq B - A$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The second is genuinely messy handwriting. The model cannot settle on
a reading and every attempt is different. That instability is the signal.

Only 3 of the 300 items had all five readings agreeing and were still scored
wrong, and all three are scoring quirks rather than real mistakes, for example
the model answering \texttt{option a} where the correct answer was written
\texttt{\textbackslash text\{A\}}. So the 0.393 accuracy is a strict lower
bound, not our best estimate: ignoring formatting gives 0.527, and accepting the
model's answer when it appears inside the correct line gives 0.640.

Importantly, \textbf{the AUROC barely moves across all of these}: 0.835 strict,
0.785 ignoring formatting, 0.777 under the loosest rule, and 0.814 when the
whole pipeline is re-run with a different \LaTeX{} parser. The signal does not
depend on how strictly we score correctness.

\subsection*{Judging mistakes: no signal, because the model cannot do the task \failed{(fails)}}

The reason matters more than the number. In FERMAT, 84.8\% of solutions contain
a mistake. On a sample with that same imbalance the model scored 75\%, which
sounds reasonable until you notice that always answering ``yes there is a
mistake'', without looking at anything, scores 87\%. The model was doing worse
than that. On a balanced set the flattery disappears:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
Accuracy on the balanced set & \textbf{0.517} \quad (chance $=0.500$) \\
\quad on solutions that do contain a mistake & 0.947 \\
\quad on solutions that are correct & \textbf{0.087} \\
Answers ``there is a mistake'' & 93\% of all items \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The model has one firm opinion, that every solution contains a mistake, and it
repeats it. So it almost never disagrees with itself: 42\% of items had all five
answers identical, and only three different entropy values appeared in the whole
run. There is nothing for entropy to measure.

\section*{What this means}

Entropy measures whether a model is consistent with itself. It never looks at
whether the model is right. The method only works when those two things go
together, meaning the model is steady when it is right and wavers when it is
wrong. That holds for reading handwriting. It fails for judging mistakes,
because a model that is confidently wrong looks exactly like one that is
confidently right. Both look steady.

The clearest evidence is a smaller result. On the solutions that were actually
correct, AUROC is \textbf{0.239}, which is below 0.5. That means entropy ran
backwards. The model was at its most consistent exactly when it was wrong,
insisting on mistakes that were not there. Low disagreement there is actively
misleading, not just uninformative.

So the headline is not that entropy fails for reasoning. It is that
\textbf{this kind of uncertainty inherits the model's ability}. It can flag
errors for a model that knows something, and it tells you nothing about a model
that is guessing. We only found this by rebalancing the test set. On the
original imbalanced data the problem was invisible.

\section*{Next step}

The judging result is about this model, not the method. The obvious test is a
stronger model (7B, or a text-only model given the correct transcription), with
one condition: check its accuracy against the 0.500 baseline first, before
computing any uncertainty numbers. If it still scores near chance, the negative
result holds for this kind of task. If it reaches roughly 0.65 to 0.70, the
question becomes answerable again and our existing code applies unchanged.

The handwriting result is usable as it stands and deserves a threshold analysis:
how precision and recall trade off as the cutoff moves, and conformal
calibration for a guaranteed error rate. That needs no new model runs.
